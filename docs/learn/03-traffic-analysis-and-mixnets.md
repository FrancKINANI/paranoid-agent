# 03 — Traffic Analysis and Mixnets

## The question

If an adversary cannot read your traffic, why does it still matter *when* you send it, and how much?

## First principle

**A message that cannot be read still has a shape, and the shape is information.**

Encryption is very good at one thing: making content opaque. It does nothing at all about timing,
volume, and direction. Those properties are not incidental — they are exactly what a network needs
in order to carry the message, and they are visible to anyone positioned to observe the wire.

The counter-intuitive consequence: **you can have perfect encryption and zero anonymity.** A TLS
session that reveals "this person sent 4 KB at 09:14 and received 800 KB at 09:14:03" is perfectly
confidential from a cryptographic standpoint, and completely transparent from a behavioural one.

## How the attack works: end-to-end correlation

Picture the two ends of a Tor connection, and an adversary who can see both.

- Your **guard relay** observes: *at 09:14:00, this client sent 4 KB upstream.*
- The **provider** observes: *at 09:14:03, I received a 4 KB prompt.*

Neither observation identifies you. But the adversary who holds both — whether because they are one
entity, because they collude, or because they are a global passive observer watching all links —
performs the simplest possible analysis: **find the request that matches this response.**

This works because real usage has a distinctive signature:

| Pattern | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| A small burst upstream, then a long sustained stream downstream | A chat request and its streamed answer |
| Thirty seconds later, the same pattern | Another turn in the same conversation |
| Nothing for two hours, then ten requests in a minute | Someone working on a task |
| Every weekday at 09:00 | Someone's routine — enough to identify them among a small population |

None of that requires reading a byte of plaintext. Notice also that it is *not* defeated by the
per-conversation circuit isolation from lesson 02: isolation prevents the *provider* and *exit* from
linking your conversations to each other. It does not prevent a party who can see both ends from
linking your traffic to *you*.

**This is the single most important limitation of the entire paranoid-mode design**, and it is the
one Vitalik Buterin named himself: an AI provider that cooperates with your guard relay can correlate
timing and defeat the protection. He proposed a mixnet as the answer. Let us examine why random cover
traffic is not.

## Why random cover traffic is weak

The intuitive fix is to add decoy traffic, so the real request is "hidden in noise". It helps, but far
less than people expect, for three reasons.

**1. Noise you can model is noise you can subtract.** If cover traffic is generated by a *published,
predictable* process — for instance, requests arriving with Poisson-distributed gaps at a known rate —
then the adversary knows the distribution of the noise. Detection becomes a statistical exercise:
given these observations, is this consistent with cover traffic alone, or cover plus a signal? More
observations mean a better answer. **A signal of 800 KB is not hidden behind decoys of 2 KB, however
many decoys there are.**

**2. Cover that does not resemble your traffic is trivially separable.** The pattern this project
explicitly rejects — "background thread sending random HTTP requests to random public sites" — produces
small GETs to unrelated destinations. A streamed model response is a multi-megabyte upload/download
pair to a provider endpoint. A classifier separates them in one line: *is the destination a known AI
provider?* The decoys are not cover; they are a labelled population of non-signals.

**3. It defends the wrong observer.** This is the crucial distinction, and it is easy to miss:

| Adversary | Does cover traffic help? |
| --- | --- |
| **Local observer** — your ISP, cafe Wi-Fi, an employer | **Yes.** They see more traffic through Tor and cannot tell which requests were real. Their problem gets harder |
| **End-to-end correlator** — provider plus guard, or a global passive observer | **No.** At both ends, your real traffic is visible *anyway*. Adding decoys at the client does not remove the signal that the provider itself received |

Cover traffic is therefore a **local mitigation**, and its value is limited to raising the cost for
observers who can only see your side of the connection. Anyone who tells you it defeats an adversary
who sees both ends is either mistaken or selling something.

That is why [../THREAT-MODEL.md](../THREAT-MODEL.md) describes this project's cover traffic as
"background noise, **not** proven traffic-analysis protection" — a phrase taken directly from
upstream's own documentation, and repeated rather than softened because it is true.

## What actually defends against correlation: constant-rate traffic

The strongest answer is not *noise* but *shape*:

> **Make every observation look the same, by transmitting at a constant rate whether or not you have
> anything to say.**

If your link carries exactly 100 KB every 250 ms, forever, then an observer learns nothing from
timing: there is no signal to correlate, because there is no variation to correlate. Your real
messages are simply the packets that happen to contain real data, indistinguishable from the ones
that do not.

This is the difference between the two strategies, and it is worth stating precisely:

| Strategy | Mechanism | Adversary's counter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Noise injection** (random cover) | Add decoys alongside the signal | Model the decoys' distribution, subtract, keep the signal |
| **Traffic shaping** (constant rate) | Remove variation entirely; the signal *becomes* the channel | None available from timing — there is no timing information left |

Constant-rate is strictly stronger and strictly more expensive: **you pay for bandwidth continuously,
including when idle.** For a person chatting with a model, that means paying for a full-rate tunnel to
say "hi" once an hour. This is why constant-rate schemes live inside mixnets rather than in ordinary
Tor usage.

## Mixnets: mixing instead of hiding

A **mixnet** combines constant-rate operation with deliberate, randomised delay. Its goal is not to
hide the message but to break the *correspondence* between what goes in and what comes out.

The mechanics, in the abstract:

1. **Fixed-size packets.** Every packet is padded to the same size, so size carries no information.
2. **Batching and independent random delays.** Each hop holds packets briefly and releases them in a
   shuffled order, adding random delay. The loop is broken.
3. **Constant-rate sending.** Clients transmit at a fixed rate; real messages are indistinguishable
   from cover because they *are* the same distribution. Loopix and the Nym network work this way;
   Katzenpost takes the same constant-rate position and openly drops most of the cover traffic it
   carries.
4. **Layered routing**, as in Tor, so no single node knows both ends.

**What it buys:** resistance to a global passive adversary — the class of attacker that Tor cannot
defend against at all. Mixing plus padding removes the timing correlation that Tor leaks.

**What it costs, honestly:**

| Cost | Why it is unavoidable |
| --- | --- |
| **Latency** | Mixing *is* delay. Randomised delay of seconds is inherent to the design, not an implementation defect. A "tuned" mixnet that is fast has weakened the property that makes it a mixnet |
| **Bandwidth** | Constant rate plus fixed-size packets plus padding means you transmit continuously, at a rate you must choose before you know your traffic |
| **Streaming fit** | Fixed-size packets are a poor shape for streaming megabytes of model output. This is a design mismatch, not a bug to fix |
| **Anonymity set** | Mixing hides you among the traffic in the mix. **If few people use it, you stand out.** A mixnet with four users protects nobody |
| **Maturity** | Research-grade, with active adversarial study — including a 2026 paper analysing attacks on Nym's reputation system |

There is a further subtlety worth knowing: a mixnet's latency is often described as a knob
("mixnet tuning") to trade anonymity for speed. That is real, but note what the knob does — it moves
you back toward Tor-like behaviour. **You cannot get mixnet-grade metadata protection at Tor-grade
latency**; the tradeoff is the design, not a tuning mistake in it.

## The honest summary of this lesson

| Approach | Protects against local observer | Protects against end-to-end correlator | Latency cost | Bandwidth cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tor only | Yes (source hidden) | No | Low | Low |
| Tor + random cover | Yes, better | **No** | Low | Moderate |
| Tor + constant-rate cover | Yes | Partially | Moderate | High (continuous) |
| Mixnet | Yes | Yes, by design | **High** | **High** |

The middle rows are where this project sits for Tiers 0–2. The bottom row is Tier 3, and it is
**unbuilt and unbenchmarked** — deliberately listed as an experiment rather than a feature.

## What this means here

- **Decision D7:** our cover traffic is bounded, Poisson-scheduled, opt-in, aimed only at permitted
  targets, and *labelled as unproven*. We adopt upstream's scheduling discipline — including that it
  drops arrivals under saturation rather than queuing or bursting — because a cover implementation
  with its own recognisable rhythm becomes a fingerprint of its own.
- **Decision D9 / open question U6:** the provider-plus-guard attack is out of scope for Tiers 0–2.
  Tier 3 would require a mixnet, with the costs in the table above. Whether that is worth pursuing is
  a question for you, and lesson 08 lays out the tradeoff.
- The **1 GiB/hour** figure you will see quoted for cover traffic (from upstream's documentation) is
  the reason our default rate is dramatically lower than theirs and the feature is off by default:
  cover has a real cost to your bandwidth and to any third party hosting the target endpoints.

## Self-check

1. Your prompts are encrypted and you use per-conversation Tor circuits. You do not use cover traffic.
   What can a well-positioned adversary still infer, and from what?
2. Cover traffic is generated with a known Poisson schedule at a known mean rate, independently of
   your actual activity. An adversary observes your link for six hours. Does the cover still help?
   Against which observer?
3. A mixnet advertises "constant-rate cover traffic, so timing analysis is impossible". What are the
   two costs you must accept, and why can neither be engineered away?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. It can infer **what you are doing and when** — that a request preceded a large streamed response,
   how many turns a conversation had, how long it lasted, and your daily rhythm. It gets that from
   timing and volume at both ends, without reading anything. Per-conversation circuits stop the
   provider from linking conversations to *each other*; they do nothing about linking traffic to *you*
   via timing.

2. It still helps against a **local** observer — they cannot tell which requests were real, so their
   confidence drops. It does **not** help against an **end-to-end** correlator: the correlation is done
   at the far end, where the real request arrived regardless of what decoys you sent from home. Six
   hours of observations also sharpen a statistical model considerably, so any gain against the local
   observer erodes with time.

3. **Latency** (mixing is randomised delay by construction) and **bandwidth** (constant rate means
   transmitting continuously at a rate chosen in advance). Neither can be engineered away because they
   are the source of the protection: remove the delay and you have Tor; remove the constant rate and
   timing information returns. A vendor promising mixnet privacy at VPN latency is describing the
   absence of the product.

</details>
